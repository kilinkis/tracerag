# Clock Management and Ten-Second Runoffs

## Illegally conserving time

After the two-minute warning of either half, the offense cannot conserve time through specified
illegal acts while the clock is running. Examples include intentional grounding, an illegal forward
pass, throwing a backward pass out of bounds, or illegally batting or kicking the ball out of
bounds.

The usual consequence for an offensive act that illegally conserves time is the applicable yardage
penalty plus a ten-second runoff. The play clock is reset to 30 seconds and the game clock starts on
the ready-for-play signal. The offense can use an available timeout instead of the runoff, and the
defense can decline the runoff. A runoff can end the half.

## Injury and excess-timeout runoffs

An excess timeout charged to the team possessing the ball can produce a ten-second runoff when it
stops a running clock or prevents the clock from starting on the ready-for-play signal. The opponent
chooses whether to apply that runoff. There is no ten-second runoff against the defensive team.

The presence of fouls, injuries to both teams, remaining timeouts, and earlier excess timeouts can
change the result. Those facts are required before giving a definitive ruling.

## Runoffs after replay

After the two-minute warning of either half, a replay reversal can require a ten-second runoff when
the corrected ruling would have left the game clock running. Neither team may decline this replay
runoff, but either team may use a remaining charged timeout to prevent it.
